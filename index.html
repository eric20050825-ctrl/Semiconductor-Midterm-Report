Strain-Engineered Bands

How squeezing or stretching a crystal rewrites its electronic and vibrational spectrum.

Mechanical strain changes the spacing between atoms. Because a semiconductor's band structure is built entirely from how neighboring atomic orbitals overlap, even a strain of a few tenths of a percent is enough to shift band edges, split degenerate valleys, and move Raman-active phonon lines — the same physics that lets foundries boost transistor mobility and lets a lab measure stress non-destructively with a laser.

Part 1 · The physics

Why atomic spacing decides the band structure

A semiconductor's energy bands are not a fixed property of its chemical formula — they are a property of the geometry its atoms happen to be sitting in. Strain changes that geometry, so it changes the bands.

1.1From bond length to bandwidth

In a simple tight-binding picture, a band forms because an electron can hop from atom to atom through orbital overlap. The hopping (transfer) integral t falls off steeply with interatomic distance d — roughly t ∝ d⁻ⁿ with n of order 2–4 for typical s/p orbitals. Since the bandwidth and the bonding–antibonding splitting both scale with t, stretching a bond (tensile strain) weakens overlap and narrows bands; compressing a bond (compressive strain) strengthens overlap and widens them.

t(d) = t₀·(d₀ / d)n,   d = d₀(1 + ε) d₀ is the equilibrium bond length, ε the applied strain along the bond.

1.2Two kinds of strain, two kinds of consequence

Any strain tensor splits into a hydrostatic part (equal in all directions — pure volume change, Tr ε = εxx+εyy+εzz) and a shear / deviatoric part (direction-dependent, zero trace). Hydrostatic strain preserves the crystal's point-group symmetry, so it can only shift energies rigidly. Shear strain breaks that symmetry, so it can split any level that was degenerate for symmetry reasons. This single distinction explains almost everything below.

ε = εhydro·𝟙 + εshear,   εhydro = 13Tr ε

1.3Deformation-potential theory

Formally, this is captured by k·p / deformation-potential theory: for a small strain, every band edge shifts linearly in ε, with a material- and band-specific proportionality constant (the deformation potential) that plays the same role a spring constant plays for a stretched bond. For the hydrostatic part of the gap:

ΔEg = agap · εhydro agap is typically a few eV and negative for most group-IV / III–V semiconductors, so tensile strain (ε>0) narrows the gap and compressive strain widens it.

This is why "tensile vs. compressive" is not just a sign flip of the same number — in multivalley or degenerate systems, the shear component the two cases carry can split bands in opposite directions even when the hydrostatic shift looks symmetric.

1.4Lifting valence-band degeneracy

At the valence-band maximum of a diamond/zinc-blende semiconductor, the heavy-hole (HH) and light-hole (LH) bands are degenerate at k = 0 by cubic symmetry. A shear strain breaks that symmetry (the Pikus–Bir / Bir–Pikus Hamiltonian formalizes this) and splits them apart, with the splitting odd in the shear strain — it vanishes for pure hydrostatic strain and grows linearly with |εshear|.

ΔEHH−LH ≈ 2b · εshear b is the valence-band shear deformation potential (a few eV). Lifting this degeneracy is exactly what reduces the in-plane hole effective mass in strained-channel PMOS.

1.5Splitting conduction-band valleys

Silicon's conduction-band minimum is not at k = 0: it sits at six equivalent points along the ⟨100⟩ directions (the Δ valleys), related to each other by the cubic symmetry of the lattice. A uniaxial strain along one ⟨100⟩ axis breaks that equivalence, splitting the six valleys into two groups — two valleys aligned with the strain axis (Δ₂) and four perpendicular to it (Δ₄) — shifting apart by an amount set by the uniaxial deformation potential Ξu.

ΔEΔ₂ = −23Ξuεshear,   ΔEΔ₄ = +13Ξuεshear Electrons preferentially occupy the lower valley group, which also has a lighter in-plane mass and suffers less intervalley scattering — the working principle of strained-Si NMOS. Valleys that sit at the Γ point (as in GaAs or GaN) are singly degenerate and have nothing to split.

1.6Phonons feel the same symmetry breaking

A lattice phonon frequency is set by a force constant and an atomic mass, ω ∝ √(k/μ). Strain softens or stiffens the bond force constant k just as it does the electronic hopping integral, giving a roughly linear "Raman shift" with hydrostatic strain. But the deeper signature of strain is the same degeneracy-lifting story as the electronic bands: a zone-center optical phonon that was doubly or triply degenerate by symmetry splits into distinct branches once shear strain breaks that symmetry — a doubly-degenerate E₂(high) mode in a hexagonal (wurtzite) crystal such as GaN splits into E₂(high)⁺ and E₂(high)⁻; the analogous triply-degenerate zone-center mode of a cubic crystal such as Si or GaAs splits into a singlet and a doublet. Because this splitting (and the accompanying shift) is measurable with a simple backscattering Raman setup, it is one of the standard non-destructive ways to map strain across a wafer.

ω±(ε) = ω₀ + 12(p+q)εhydro ± 12r·εshear p, q, r are the phonon deformation potentials (Anastassakis formalism); the ± branches coincide exactly when εshear = 0.
